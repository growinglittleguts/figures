The Living Microbiome

Where the immune training shows up

The gut bacteria that settle in during your baby's first months train an immune system that reaches nearly every organ. Here is where that training turns into health outcomes.

~70% of immune cells live in the gut

Breathing and asthma risk

Gut bacteria in the first 100 days track with childhood asthma risk — four protective species, present or missing, show up in the data years later.

How it works

Short-chain fatty acids made by gut bacteria travel in the bloodstream and change how immune cells in the lungs behave. Regulatory T cells trained in the gut migrate to the airways.

Eczema and food allergy

Babies with Bifidobacterium-dominant guts at one month old have the lowest rates of eczema and food sensitization.

How it works

Gut-derived short-chain fatty acids feed skin cells and strengthen the skin barrier — where allergic sensitization often begins. How antibodies coat gut bacteria in infancy predicts allergy years later.

Mood and neurodevelopment

The same early signals influence neurodevelopment, mood regulation, and how your baby's stress response gets calibrated.

How it works

The vagus nerve carries signals directly from gut to brain. Bacteria make serotonin precursors from dietary tryptophan — about 95% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut.

Growth and metabolism

Early microbiome shifts affect growth outcomes, appetite signaling, and how efficiently your baby's body handles energy.

How it works

Bacteria process bile acids, which sets how much fat gets absorbed. Short-chain fatty acids signal to fat cells and gut hormone cells, affecting insulin sensitivity and appetite hormones.

Lifelong immune memory

Immune education in the first three years has lasting effects that later exposure cannot fully replace.

How it works

Microbial exposure epigenetically reprograms innate immune cells — memory that lasts months to years. Even the inflammation that comes with starting solids is necessary for adult immune competence.

None of this is fixed at birth. The window stays open for about three years, and what happens inside it — the foods your baby meets, the environments they live in — helps set the trajectory.
